How to Clean Puja Room Before Diwali: Step-by-Step Guide

Here is how to clean puja room shelves, idols and lamps before Diwali: work from the top shelf down, clean each item with the mildest cleaner that works on its material, and dry everything fully before it goes back. Lemon that brightens a brass diya will etch a marble mandir, so how to clean puja room items safely starts with sorting them by material. This guide on how to clean puja room fittings follows a simple order: idols and lamps, mandir wood, walls and floor, then storing diyas and decor. If you also need to know how to clean puja room corners for Navratri or a Golu display, begin two to three weeks before Dhanteras (Friday 6 November in 2026). We cover cleaning and care only; follow your family's own custom for anything ritual, such as moving idols.

What each material can and cannot take

Most damage in a puja room comes from using one cleaner on everything. Check the material first.

Step 1: Clear the shelf and dust dry

Spread a clean bedsheet on a table near the mandir and move every item onto it, grouped by material: brass and copper together, silver apart, marble and stone in one corner, clay and painted pieces in another. Photograph the arrangement first so it goes back the same way.

Before anything gets wet, dust the empty mandir from the top canopy down with a soft paintbrush for the carvings and a dry microfibre cloth for flat surfaces. Clear out old flowers, dry garlands, incense ash, used matchsticks and stale offerings. Look behind the mandir while it is empty. Sweet offerings draw ants, and a steady line along the wall usually means a nest nearby. Our ant control service treats the source without spraying near your idols.

Step 2: Clean the idols

Metal idols

Wash brass, copper and panchdhatu idols in a basin of warm water with a few drops of dish soap, using a soft toothbrush for faces, jewellery details and the base. Then use the table method for the metal, rinse in clean water and pat dry with a cotton cloth. Hollow idols often have an opening in the base. Stand them upside down on a towel for a few hours so trapped water drains, or it leaves green or black marks.

Marble and stone idols

Wipe marble idols with a cloth dipped in warm water and mild soap, rinse with a second damp cloth and dry at once. Kumkum and haldi stains need patience rather than force: make a thick paste of baking soda and water, spread it over the stain, cover it with cling film and leave it for a few hours before wiping off. Black stone idols need only a soft brush, water and a dry cloth.

Painted, clay and fabric pieces

Clay and plaster idols stay dry. Use a soft makeup brush or a clean paintbrush and stop if you see paint lifting. Hand wash the vastra, chunri and small cushions separately in cold water, because red and yellow dyes often bleed, and check for sequins or zari that should not be wrung.

Step 3: Lamps, diyas, bells and thali

Oil lamps carry the heaviest build-up. Scrape out soft ghee and burnt wick, then soak the diya in hot soapy water for 20 to 30 minutes before you treat the metal. For brass, rub half a lemon dipped in salt over the dull areas, or spread tamarind pulp and leave it for ten minutes, then rinse well. Candle wax lifts more easily if you harden it with an ice cube first and slide it off with an old plastic card.

Silver thalis and small bowls respond well to a gentle dip: line a steel bowl with aluminium foil, add a spoon of baking soda and hot water, rest the silver on the foil for a few minutes and the tarnish loosens. Skip this for antique pieces with a deliberately darkened finish and anything with stones or glued parts. Copper kalash and lota interiors need a bottle brush with lemon and salt and a thorough rinse. Wipe the ghanti with a damp cloth rather than soaking it.

Step 4: Clean the mandir wood

A wooden mandir is furniture, and the same rules for how to clean wooden furniture at home apply, only with more oil and soot. Mix warm water with a few drops of dish soap, dip a microfibre cloth and wring it until it is barely damp. Wipe along the grain, one panel at a time, and follow straight away with a dry cloth. Ghee drips come off with the same soapy cloth if you blot the fresh oil first. Soot under the top canopy should be lifted with a dry sponge before any water touches it, or it smears into a grey film.

MDF and laminate mandirs swell if water reaches the edges, so keep the cloth even drier there. Once the wood has dried for a few hours, apply a small amount of furniture polish suited to the finish, testing first on the back panel. While the mandir is empty, tap the panels and check the back for fine powder or mud lines on the wall behind it. Either can mean termites, which are worth a termite inspection before you put everything back.

Step 5: Walls, tiles and floor

The wall above the diya spot usually carries a dark patch. Treat it dry first with a soot sponge or a clean dry cloth, then wipe gently with mild soap if your paint is washable; test a hidden corner. Our guide to cleaning house walls by paint type covers matt and emulsion finishes in detail.

For a marble floor, sweep first so grit does not scratch, then mop with a pH-neutral cleaner and dry the surface with a cloth. Fresh ghee or oil spills come up if you cover them with talcum powder or baking soda for an hour, sweep it away, then wash with mild soap. See our marble floor care guide for routine care; dull or etched patches that cleaning cannot fix are a job for professional marble polishing.

Step 6: Store diyas and decor

Everything must be completely dry before storage, and metal pieces with crevices need a full day in the air. Wrap brass and copper in soft cotton cloth. Store silver in zip-lock bags with a small sachet of silica gel, away from rubber bands and rubber-lined boxes, which speed up tarnish. Clay diyas crack in damp cupboards, so wrap them in newspaper and keep the box on a high, dry shelf.

Keep camphor in an airtight container on a high shelf: it can be harmful if swallowed, and babies, children and pets should never be able to reach it. Matches and spare incense belong in a closed tin, away from the lamps.

Navratri puja room cleaning and Golu tips

Navratri falls a few weeks before Diwali, so plan one thorough clean before Navratri and a light touch-up before Dhanteras rather than two full cleans.

For a Golu, wipe the wooden steps with a barely damp cloth and check the joints for borer holes or powder. Clay Golu dolls should only be brushed dry; look for hairline cracks, then wrap each doll in tissue or newspaper with a little silica gel and pack them in boxes labelled by step.

Mistakes to avoid while cleaning wooden furniture and fittings

These are the process mistakes we see most often:

  1. Spraying cleaner straight onto wood. It pools in joints and carvings and lifts the finish. Spray the cloth instead.
  2. Polishing over dust. Grit trapped under a polish cloth leaves fine scratches. Dust, wipe, dry, then polish.
  3. Leaving a lit diya close under a wooden canopy. Heat and soot mark the wood. Keep a clear gap above the flame and never leave a lamp burning near garlands or curtains with nobody in the room.
  4. Mixing cleaners. Never combine bleach with ammonia or with acidic cleaners; the fumes are dangerous. Ventilate the room, wear gloves and use one product at a time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use lemon to clean a marble mandir?

No. Lemon juice and vinegar are acids, and marble is calcium carbonate, so even a short contact leaves dull, rough patches called etching. Use a pH-neutral cleaner or a few drops of mild soap in warm water, and for haldi or kumkum stains use a baking soda paste left on for a few hours.

How do I remove black oil soot from a brass diya?

Scrape off the soft residue first, then soak the diya in hot water with dish soap for 20 to 30 minutes. Rub the remaining black marks with lemon and salt or tamarind pulp, rinse in clean water and dry it fully with a cotton cloth so new water spots do not form.

How often should a puja room be deep cleaned?

A light daily wipe of the lamp area and a weekly dusting keep build-up low. A full clean of idols, metalware, wood and floor two or three times a year suits most homes, typically before Navratri or Diwali and once mid-year.

Is it safe to keep camphor and incense in the puja room with small children at home?

Yes, if you store them properly. Camphor can be harmful if swallowed, so keep it in a closed container on a high shelf, and keep matches and incense in a lidded tin. Never leave a burning diya or incense unattended where a child or pet can reach it.
